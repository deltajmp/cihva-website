Overexertion & Lifting

Overview

The leading cause of EMS injury. Repeated heavy lifts in awkward positions — stairwells, tight hallways, unstable surfaces — place extreme load on the spine and shoulders.

Who's at Risk

All providers, but especially those with 5+ years on the job due to cumulative wear.

Common Injuries

Lumbar disc herniation, muscle strains, rotator cuff tears.

Key Stat: A loaded stretcher can weigh 300–700+ lbs. EMS workers may perform 5–10 significant lifts per shift.
Prevention: Always adjust stretcher height before loading. Request lift assist when needed. Never rush a lift.

Slips, Trips & Falls

Overview

Scene environments are unpredictable — wet floors, uneven terrain, dark stairwells, ice, and debris all create fall hazards.

Who's at Risk

Providers responding at night or in adverse weather.

Common Injuries

Ankle sprains, knee ligament tears, wrist fractures, head injuries.

Key Stat: Falls account for a disproportionate share of lost work days compared to their overall frequency.
Prevention: Slow down on approach. Use a flashlight. Plant your feet before lifting. Wear slip-resistant footwear.

Vehicle Incidents

Overview

Both ambulance crashes and being struck while working near roadways. Intersection collisions are the #1 cause of EMS fatalities.

Who's at Risk

Drivers and providers working highway or MVC scenes.

Common Injuries

Whiplash, traumatic brain injury, fractures, fatalities.

Key Stat: EMS workers are 2.5× more likely to die in a vehicle incident than the average worker.
Prevention: Always wear your seatbelt in the ambulance — even in the back. At roadway scenes, position the unit as a barrier and wear high-visibility gear.

Struck by Object

Overview

Equipment falling from overhead storage, stretcher components releasing unexpectedly, or objects at chaotic scenes.

Who's at Risk

Providers working in tight quarters or with aging equipment.

Common Injuries

Hand and wrist fractures, eye injuries, lacerations, head trauma.

Key Stat: Ambulance interior equipment is responsible for a significant portion of struck-by incidents.
Prevention: Inspect all latches and locks at the start of every shift. Keep cabinets closed when the unit is in motion.

Assault & Violence

Overview

EMS providers are frequently exposed to patients or bystanders who are combative due to psychiatric crisis, intoxication, or conditions like hypoglycemia or hypoxia.

Who's at Risk

All providers — violence is the #1 underreported EMS injury.

Common Injuries

Soft tissue injuries, bites, eye injuries, psychological trauma.

Key Stat: Studies suggest up to 90% of EMS violence incidents go unreported.
Prevention: Position yourself near exits. Trust your instincts. Report every incident — data drives policy change.

Other / Miscellaneous

Overview

Includes needle sticks, chemical exposures, repetitive strain from documentation and driving posture, and hearing loss from siren exposure.

Who's at Risk

Providers across all roles and experience levels.

Common Injuries

Bloodborne pathogen exposure, carpal tunnel, chronic hearing loss, skin conditions.

Key Stat: Hearing loss is one of the most overlooked long-term occupational hazards in EMS.
Prevention: Wear PPE consistently. Use ergonomic positioning while driving. Get regular hearing checks.

Shift Fatigue

12–24 hour shifts impair reaction time, judgment, and physical coordination — dramatically increasing injury risk toward the end of shift.

Unpredictable Lifts

Unlike warehouse workers, EMS providers can't control lift conditions. Every scene is different — floors, angles, patient size, and bystander help all vary.

Scene Hazards

Homes, roadsides, and public spaces present hazards that can't be engineered away. Awareness and preparation are the only defenses.

Chronic Stress Load

Psychological stress tightens muscles, impairs posture, and reduces body awareness — all of which contribute to physical injury over time.

Cervical Spine
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Cervical muscle strain from sudden patient movements
  • Cervical disc herniation from repetitive awkward positioning
  • Facet joint irritation
  • Tension headaches from sustained neck flexion during patient care
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers frequently work in cramped spaces — leaning over patients in back seats, kneeling in hallways, or looking down during prolonged airway management. Sustained neck flexion compresses cervical discs and strains posterior muscles. Heavy helmet and radio equipment add compressive load.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Pain or stiffness not resolving after 48–72 hours of rest
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness radiating into the arm or hand
  • Headaches originating at the base of the skull
  • Clicking or grinding with neck movement
These symptoms may indicate disc or nerve involvement. See a provider — do not push through.
Prevention Strategies
  • Keep your chin tucked — not jutted forward — during patient care
  • Reposition yourself rather than twisting your neck to see
  • Strengthen deep cervical flexors: chin tucks, neck isometrics
  • Take micro-breaks to roll and decompress the neck during long calls
  • Adjust stretcher height so you're not constantly looking down
Shoulders
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Rotator cuff strain or tear — supraspinatus most common
  • Shoulder impingement syndrome
  • AC joint sprain from falls or carrying heavy equipment
  • Bicep tendinopathy
  • SLAP tears from repetitive overhead or loading motions
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

Loading patients overhead into high ambulances, reaching across stretchers, carrying equipment bags, and CPR compressions all place repetitive high-load demand on the rotator cuff. The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body — and mobility comes at the cost of stability.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Pain when reaching overhead or behind your back
  • Weakness when lifting — unable to hold arm at shoulder height
  • Painful arc between 60–120 degrees of arm raise
  • Night pain that wakes you from sleep
Rotator cuff injuries worsen significantly if ignored. Early intervention prevents surgical outcomes.
Prevention Strategies
  • Strengthen rotator cuff: external rotation exercises with resistance bands
  • Never load a cot or equipment overhead solo — use proper technique or request help
  • Keep elbows close to the body during lifts
  • Stretch pecs and anterior shoulder to prevent forward shoulder posture
  • Avoid reaching across your body with a loaded arm
Lumbar Spine
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Lumbar disc herniation — L4-L5 and L5-S1 most common in EMS
  • Lumbar muscle and ligament sprain
  • Sacroiliac joint dysfunction
  • Lumbar facet syndrome
  • Degenerative disc disease accelerated by cumulative loading
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

The lumbar spine is the most injured region in EMS. Heavy, unpredictable lifts from flexed or rotated positions — especially with a loaded stretcher — create compressive and shear forces that exceed safe spinal load thresholds. Fatigue, poor lift mechanics, and time pressure multiply the risk.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Sharp pain during or after a lift that doesn't resolve within a shift
  • Pain radiating down one or both legs (sciatica)
  • Numbness or tingling in legs, feet, or groin
  • Inability to stand fully upright after bending
Loss of bladder or bowel control is a medical emergency — seek care immediately. Disc herniations and nerve compression require prompt evaluation.
Prevention Strategies
  • Hinge at the hips — not the waist — on every lift
  • Keep load close to your body at all times
  • Adjust stretcher height before lifting — never lift from ground level when avoidable
  • Strengthen the posterior chain: glutes, hamstrings, and erectors
  • Core stability: dead bugs, planks, bird dogs — not sit-ups
  • Use mechanical lift assist or additional crew for bariatric patients
Knees
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Patellar tendinopathy from repetitive kneeling on hard surfaces
  • Meniscus tears from twisting while supporting a loaded patient
  • MCL/LCL sprains from falls or lateral forces at uneven scenes
  • IT band syndrome from repetitive stair climbing
  • Prepatellar bursitis from prolonged kneeling
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers kneel on hard floors, concrete, and asphalt regularly during patient care. Stair climbing with loaded stair chairs places significant eccentric load on the patellar tendon and quadriceps. Pivoting while supporting a patient creates dangerous rotational forces on the knee joint.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Swelling around the kneecap or joint line
  • Locking or catching sensation in the knee
  • Inability to fully straighten or bend the knee
  • Pain descending stairs more than ascending
  • Instability or "giving way" feeling
Locking and instability suggest meniscal or ligament involvement — do not delay evaluation.
Prevention Strategies
  • Use a kneeling pad or kneel on one knee rather than both when working on the floor
  • Strengthen quads and glutes to protect the joint
  • Avoid twisting your knee while bearing weight
  • Communicate steps clearly with your partner during stair chair operations
  • Stretch hip flexors and calves — tightness above and below affects knee tracking
Wrists & Hands
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome from repetitive documentation and steering
  • De Quervain's tenosynovitis — thumb-side wrist pain from gripping
  • Scaphoid fracture from catching a fall with an outstretched hand
  • Trigger finger from repetitive gripping of equipment and bags
  • Hand lacerations and crush injuries at chaotic scenes
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

Sustained gripping of stretcher handles, stair chairs, and equipment bags — combined with hours of documentation on tablets or keyboards — creates repetitive strain on wrist tendons and the median nerve. Falls frequently result in wrist fractures as providers instinctively reach out to catch themselves.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Numbness or tingling in the thumb, index, or middle fingers — especially at night
  • Pain with wrist extension or gripping
  • Swelling or bruising after a fall — always rule out scaphoid fracture
  • Weakness in grip strength
Scaphoid fractures can appear normal on initial X-ray. Always follow up persistent wrist pain after a fall.
Prevention Strategies
  • Use ergonomic grips or gloves for equipment handling
  • Take wrist stretch breaks during documentation-heavy periods
  • Wrist flexor and extensor stretches — hold 30 seconds each direction
  • Strengthen grip and wrist extensors with resistance exercises
  • When falling, try to tuck and roll rather than reaching out
Ankles & Feet
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Lateral ankle sprain — most common EMS lower extremity injury
  • Plantar fasciitis from prolonged standing on hard surfaces
  • Achilles tendinopathy
  • Stress fractures from high-volume calls on hard surfaces
  • Metatarsalgia from poorly fitting boots
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers navigate uneven terrain, stairs, wet surfaces, and debris — often while carrying equipment or supporting patients. Long shifts on hard floors accelerate plantar fascia fatigue. Heavy, ill-fitting boots impair proprioception and ankle stability.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Ankle pain and swelling immediately after a misstep or fall
  • Heel pain worst with the first steps in the morning — plantar fasciitis hallmark
  • Achilles pain or tightness that worsens with activity
  • Localized bone pain that increases with activity
Ottawa Ankle Rules: if you can't bear weight or have bony point tenderness, get imaging.
Prevention Strategies
  • Wear properly fitted, supportive boots with ankle coverage
  • Replace boots every 12 months — outsole wear affects joint protection
  • Stretch calves and plantar fascia daily
  • Ankle stability exercises: single-leg balance, resistance band work
  • Use high-quality insoles if standing for prolonged periods
  • Slow down on approach to scenes — never rush across unknown terrain
Section 03 — Technique & Ergonomics

Lifting & Patient Handling

Proper body mechanics save careers. Step through each technique and learn the exact sequence that protects your spine, shoulders, and knees on every call.

Step 01
Size Up Before You Touch Anything

Before approaching the patient, assess the environment. Note floor surface, available space, obstacles, doorways, and your exit path. This 10-second assessment prevents the majority of lift-related injuries.

Identify wet, uneven, or cluttered surfaces
Locate the nearest clear path to the ambulance
Determine if additional help is needed before lifting
Check stretcher wheel locks and loading mechanism
Common Mistake
Rushing directly to the patient without assessing the environment — this is how falls and awkward emergency lifts happen.
Step 02
Position the Stretcher Correctly

Adjust the stretcher to its lowest stable working height before patient transfer. Align it parallel to the surface the patient is on. Both providers should be positioned before transfer begins.

Stretcher height: mid-thigh of the shorter provider
Confirm all wheel locks are engaged before transfer
Clear the path before moving the patient
Never lift a patient onto a stretcher at the wrong height
Common Mistake
Leaving the stretcher at full height and lifting the patient up — this creates an overhead lift that dramatically increases spinal load.
Step 03
Establish Your Power Position

Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, one foot slightly forward for a stable base. Tighten your core, keep your back straight and chest up. Hinge at the hips — not the waist.

Feet shoulder-width apart — never feet together
Back straight, natural lumbar curve maintained
Core engaged before any movement begins
Knees track over toes — do not let them cave inward
Common Mistake
Rounding the lower back to reach the patient — a flexed spine under load is the primary mechanism of disc herniation.
Step 04
Grip and Load Distribution

Grip the stretcher frame — not the mattress. Keep the load as close to your body as possible. Communicate clearly with your partner about who leads the count and who controls which end.

Full overhand grip on the frame — thumbs wrapped around
Elbows tucked in — not flared out
Heavier end (torso) assigned to stronger provider
Agree on count cadence: "Ready? Lift on 3 — 1, 2, 3"
Common Mistake
Gripping only with fingers — this leads to dropped loads and sudden reactive muscle strains.
Step 05
The Lift

On the agreed count, rise by driving through your heels and extending hips and knees simultaneously. Keep the load close. Move smoothly — no jerking. If something feels wrong, call it and set down immediately.

Drive upward with your legs — not your back
Rise at the same pace as your partner
Keep eyes forward and chin up throughout
Communicate: "holding," "step," "set down"
Common Mistake
One provider lifting faster than the other — this tilts the load and forces spinal compensation.
Step 06
Loading Into the Ambulance

Both providers move to the loading end. Engage the undercarriage loading mechanism. One guides the head-end, one manages the wheels. Push — do not lift — using body weight.

Use the mechanical loading assist if your unit has one
Keep your back straight while pushing — not hunched
Confirm stretcher is fully locked before transport
Close and latch ambulance doors before moving
Common Mistake
One provider loading solo while the other documents — solo loading is a leading cause of back injury.

Step 01
Patient Assessment & Chair Setup

Confirm the patient can be safely moved in a seated position. Assess stairwell width, lighting, handrail availability, and step condition. Set up the chair with footrests secured and seatbelt fastened before moving.

Verify spine is not a concern before seating the patient
Walk the stairwell before moving — count the steps
Identify landing areas for rest points
Request a third provider for stairs longer than one flight
Common Mistake
Moving too fast down the stairs — speed is the enemy of stair chair safety.
Step 02
Positioning on the Stairs

The lower provider (facing down the stairs) controls descent and bears primary load. The upper provider guides and assists. Both providers face the direction of travel.

Lower provider: back straight, handles at hip height
Upper provider: hands on chair back, ready to control speed
Agree on step cadence before moving
Maintain communication throughout the descent
Common Mistake
The upper provider letting go to grab the handrail — both hands must stay on the chair.
Step 03
Controlled Descent Technique

Move one step at a time in a controlled, synchronized rhythm. Lower provider steps down, plants both feet, then signals before the next step. Never allow the chair to gain momentum.

One step at a time — no skipping
Full foot contact on each step before moving
Short deliberate steps — not long reaches
Call out obstacles, loose carpet, or wet steps immediately
Common Mistake
Leaning back to counterbalance the weight — this overloads the lumbar spine of the lower provider.
Step 04
Rest and Reset at Landings

At every landing, pause completely. Set the chair down, release the weight, shake out your grip, and reassess. Check on the patient. Do not push through fatigue on stairs.

Set chair down completely — do not just shift weight
Communicate with your partner and patient
If fatigued, request relief before continuing
Reassess remaining stairs before proceeding
Common Mistake
Skipping landings to move faster — fatigue in the final stretch causes the most injuries.
Step 05
Transfer to Stretcher

Once at the bottom, transfer the patient to the stretcher before moving outside if possible. The stair chair is not designed for outdoor terrain. Set stretcher at lowest position for the transfer.

Lock stair chair wheels before transfer
Three-count transfer with clear communication
Have stretcher positioned and locked before beginning
Document any patient discomfort during movement
Common Mistake
Wheeling the stair chair across gravel or uneven ground — this risks tipping the chair.

Step 01
Approach and Scene Setup

A floor patient is one of the highest-risk lift scenarios in EMS. Before touching the patient, maximize working conditions: clear the area, move furniture, improve lighting, and get enough people.

Minimum 2 providers — 3 preferred for larger patients
Clear a 3-foot working radius around the patient
Bring stretcher to its absolute lowest position
Identify the lead provider who will call all movements
Common Mistake
Attempting a floor lift with 2 providers and no room to work — this is the setup for serious injury.
Step 02
Log Roll to Position

If the patient needs repositioning, use a coordinated log roll. One provider controls the head and calls the movement. Providers maintain spinal alignment. Roll on the count — not before.

Head provider calls all movements — others follow
Keep the patient's spine in neutral alignment
Use a draw sheet or slide sheet when available
Position stretcher on the side patient will roll toward
Common Mistake
Rolling without a coordinated count — uneven movement strains providers and may injure the patient.
Step 03
Scoop or Slide to Stretcher

Use a scoop stretcher or long board whenever possible. If lifting manually, each provider takes a designated body segment. No one lifts alone. The lead calls the count.

Scoop stretcher dramatically reduces provider injury risk
Assign: head/shoulders, torso/hips, legs — one each
Everyone lifts simultaneously on the count
Transfer height no more than 12 inches — keep it low
Common Mistake
Two providers lifting a floor patient without equipment — nearly always results in one provider overloading their back.
Step 04
Managing Awkward Positions

Patients are rarely in ideal positions. In confined spaces, modify the technique: use a carry chair, a flat carry, or request additional personnel rather than forcing a dangerous lift.

Never force a lift that doesn't feel safe — stop and reassess
In bathrooms: remove the door if needed for space
Use a rescue carry for very confined spaces
Verbal communication is even more critical in tight spaces
Common Mistake
Adapting a standard technique to an impossible space — recognize when you need a completely different approach.
Step 05
Post-Lift Stretcher Adjustment

Once the patient is on the stretcher, immediately raise it to transport height before moving. Pushing a loaded stretcher at floor level is a major cause of back strain. Confirm all locks before moving.

Raise stretcher immediately — never transport at floor level
Confirm patient is secured with straps before raising
Check all four wheel locks after height adjustment
Reassess patient comfort before moving toward ambulance
Common Mistake
Moving a loaded stretcher at floor level because "it's just a short distance" — cumulative low-level pushing injuries are significant.

Step 01
When to Use a Two-Person Carry

A two-person carry is appropriate for short distances or spaces where equipment cannot access. It is NOT appropriate for suspected spinal injuries or when any other option exists.

Use only when stretcher and chair cannot access the space
Confirm no spinal injury concern before using
Keep carry distance as short as possible — to stretcher only
Communicate the route and obstacles before lifting
Common Mistake
Using a two-person carry as a default because it's faster — this is one of the highest injury-risk techniques in EMS.
Step 02
Extremity Carry Setup

One provider cradles the upper body from behind — arms under the patient's armpits, hands clasped across the chest. The second provider faces forward holding both legs at the knees. Both establish power position before lifting.

Rear provider: elbows in, back straight
Front provider: patient's knees over your forearms — not your wrists
Patient's arms crossed over chest to reduce flailing
Agree on carry height before lifting
Common Mistake
Front provider holding at the ankles instead of under the knees — this hyperextends the patient's legs and destabilizes the carry.
Step 03
Synchronized Movement

The rear provider leads and calls all movements. Move in the direction the rear provider walks — backward. Short, deliberate steps. Call out all obstacles, steps, and direction changes.

Rear provider calls: "Ready? Walk on 3 — 1, 2, 3"
Maintain consistent carry height — no bouncing
Front provider watches for obstacles rear cannot see
Stop and rest rather than rushing through fatigue
Common Mistake
Moving too fast — hurrying causes stumbles and sudden load shifts that injure providers.
Step 04
Set-Down and Transfer

Set the patient down in a controlled, coordinated movement — never drop or lower suddenly. Agree on the set-down surface before the carry begins. Lower together on the count throughout the descent.

Identify and clear the set-down surface before carrying
Lower together — do not let one provider reach ground first
Maintain power position during the entire lowering phase
Confirm patient is safe before releasing grip
Common Mistake
One provider setting down before the other — this sudden load shift is a major mechanism of injury.

Step 01
Early Recognition and Planning

Bariatric calls require a fundamentally different operational approach — not just more people doing the same thing. Identify early, plan early, and request resources early. A loaded bariatric stretcher can exceed 1,000 lbs.

Request bariatric unit and equipment before arrival if known
Notify receiving facility early for preparation
Minimum 4 providers for any bariatric lift — 6 preferred
Identify the heaviest distribution point (usually the torso)
Common Mistake
Arriving with a standard stretcher and improvising — this is the leading cause of multi-provider injury events.
Step 02
Equipment Verification

Confirm all equipment weight ratings before use. Standard stretchers are rated for 700 lbs in most cases — verify your unit's rating. Use a bariatric stretcher, scoop, or transfer board. Never exceed equipment weight ratings.

Check stretcher weight rating placard before every call
Bariatric scoop stretchers allow safer floor transfers
Slider boards dramatically reduce friction
Hospital beds can often raise to stretcher height — use this
Common Mistake
Assuming standard equipment will be fine — equipment failure mid-lift is catastrophic.
Step 03
Team Assignment

Assign specific roles before the lift. Designate a lead who calls all movements. Assign providers to head, each side of torso, and legs. Brief everyone on the technique and path before approaching the patient.

Lead provider: calls count, sets pace, monitors everyone
Never have providers improvise position mid-lift
Assign strongest providers to the torso segment
Conduct a verbal dry run before the actual lift
Common Mistake
Starting without clear team assignment — this creates confusion, uneven loading, and provider injury.
Step 04
Modified Lift Mechanics

With bariatric patients, strict power position is even more critical. Wider stance for stability. Prefer lateral transfer over vertical lift whenever surface heights allow. Use sliding and rolling rather than lifting.

Lateral transfer whenever surface heights allow
Use friction-reducing slide sheets for lateral moves
If vertical lift required: all providers lift simultaneously
Communicate fatigue immediately — no heroics
Common Mistake
Attempting a vertical lift when a lateral slide was possible — always choose the lower-force option.
Step 05
Transport and Unloading

Secure the patient with appropriately rated bariatric straps. Confirm stretcher locks at every height change. Unloading at the hospital requires the same full team — do not assume hospital staff are trained or available to assist.

Verify bariatric straps are rated to patient weight
Brief hospital staff on unloading technique before starting
Raise stretcher to highest position before unloading
Never unload a bariatric stretcher with fewer than 4 providers
Common Mistake
Unloading with whoever is available at the hospital rather than waiting for your full team — ED chaos is not a reason to cut corners.

Top 10 Lifting Mistakes That Cause EMS Injuries
01
Rushing the Lift
Time pressure is the #1 reason EMS providers skip safety steps. A 30-second setup prevents a 3-month recovery.
02
Lifting With the Back
Flexing the spine under load is the primary mechanism of lumbar disc herniation. Hinge at the hips — every time.
03
Feet Too Close Together
A narrow base during a lift forces lateral compensation that strains the spine and knees.
04
Holding Your Breath
The Valsalva maneuver spikes intra-abdominal pressure. Exhale steadily through the exertion phase.
05
Not Communicating the Count
Asynchronous lifts create sudden uneven loads. Always agree on a count before lifting.
06
Overestimating Patient Assist
A patient who says they can help often cannot. Plan for dead weight — anything extra is a bonus.
07
Loading Solo
One-person stretcher loading is a leading cause of back injury. Always use a partner or mechanical assist.
08
Ignoring Equipment Issues
A stretcher with a sticky wheel turns a routine lift into an emergency. Check equipment every shift.
09
Not Asking for Help
Requesting lift assist is a professional decision — not a sign of weakness. Your back has to last your career.
10
Pushing Through Pain
Lifting through existing pain accelerates injury. Report it, modify duties, and get evaluated.

Pre-Shift Warm-Up Routine

Cold muscles under sudden load are significantly more vulnerable to strain and tear. A 5–7 minute warm-up before your first call is one of the highest-return injury prevention habits you can build.

Hip Circles
Hip flexors, lumbar spine

Stand feet shoulder-width apart, hands on hips. Make slow controlled circles with your hips — 5 in each direction. Keep upper body still.

10 circles each direction
Cat-Cow Stretch
Thoracic and lumbar spine

On hands and knees, alternate between arching your back toward the ceiling and dropping your belly toward the floor. Move slowly and breathe through each position.

10 slow repetitions
Leg Swings
Hip flexors, hamstrings, glutes

Stand beside a wall for balance. Swing one leg forward and back in a controlled arc. Keep your standing leg slightly bent.

15 swings each leg
Shoulder Rolls & Cross-Body Stretch
Rotator cuff, posterior shoulder, trapezius

Roll shoulders backward 10 times. Then pull one arm across your chest with the opposite hand, holding 20 seconds each side.

10 rolls + 20 sec hold each side
Bodyweight Squat
Quadriceps, glutes, lumbar stabilizers

Feet shoulder-width apart, toes slightly out. Lower until thighs are parallel to the floor, chest up, back straight. Drive through heels to rise. This is your power lift rehearsal.

10 slow controlled reps
Wrist Flexor & Extensor Stretch
Forearm muscles, carpal tunnel region

Extend one arm, palm up. Use other hand to gently pull fingers downward — hold 20 seconds. Flip palm down and pull fingers upward — hold 20 seconds. Switch arms.

20 seconds each direction, each hand
Pro Tip: This routine takes less than 7 minutes. Do it at the start of every shift — before the first call, not after. Reactive warm-ups don't work.
Equipment Check Protocol

Faulty equipment is a direct cause of provider injury. A stretcher that fails mid-lift, a wheel lock that doesn't hold, or a latch that gives way can end a career. This check takes 4 minutes and should happen every shift.

0/12 items checked
Stretcher
All four wheel locks engage and release cleanly
Loading mechanism operates smoothly
Stretcher height adjusts through all positions
Safety rails lock securely up and down
Patient straps present, undamaged, buckles function
Undercarriage free of visible damage
Stair Chair
Wheel locks function on all wheels
Seatbelt present and buckle functions
Footrests fold and lock in position
Frame shows no cracks, bends, or weld failures
Bariatric Equipment (if applicable)
Bariatric stretcher weight rating placard visible
Bariatric straps rated and present
If any item fails — remove the equipment from service immediately and notify your supervisor. Never operate faulty equipment or work around a mechanical issue.
