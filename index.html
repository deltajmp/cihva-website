Overexertion & Lifting

Overview

The leading cause of EMS injury. Repeated heavy lifts in awkward positions — stairwells, tight hallways, unstable surfaces — place extreme load on the spine and shoulders.

Who's at Risk

All providers, but especially those with 5+ years on the job due to cumulative wear.

Common Injuries

Lumbar disc herniation, muscle strains, rotator cuff tears.

Key Stat: A loaded stretcher can weigh 300–700+ lbs. EMS workers may perform 5–10 significant lifts per shift.
Prevention: Always adjust stretcher height before loading. Request lift assist when needed. Never rush a lift.

Slips, Trips & Falls

Overview

Scene environments are unpredictable — wet floors, uneven terrain, dark stairwells, ice, and debris all create fall hazards.

Who's at Risk

Providers responding at night or in adverse weather.

Common Injuries

Ankle sprains, knee ligament tears, wrist fractures, head injuries.

Key Stat: Falls account for a disproportionate share of lost work days compared to their overall frequency.
Prevention: Slow down on approach. Use a flashlight. Plant your feet before lifting. Wear slip-resistant footwear.

Vehicle Incidents

Overview

Both ambulance crashes and being struck while working near roadways. Intersection collisions are the #1 cause of EMS fatalities.

Who's at Risk

Drivers and providers working highway or MVC scenes.

Common Injuries

Whiplash, traumatic brain injury, fractures, fatalities.

Key Stat: EMS workers are 2.5× more likely to die in a vehicle incident than the average worker.
Prevention: Always wear your seatbelt in the ambulance — even in the back. At roadway scenes, position the unit as a barrier and wear high-visibility gear.

Struck by Object

Overview

Equipment falling from overhead storage, stretcher components releasing unexpectedly, or objects at chaotic scenes.

Who's at Risk

Providers working in tight quarters or with aging equipment.

Common Injuries

Hand and wrist fractures, eye injuries, lacerations, head trauma.

Key Stat: Ambulance interior equipment is responsible for a significant portion of struck-by incidents.
Prevention: Inspect all latches and locks at the start of every shift. Keep cabinets closed when the unit is in motion.

Assault & Violence

Overview

EMS providers are frequently exposed to patients or bystanders who are combative due to psychiatric crisis, intoxication, or conditions like hypoglycemia or hypoxia.

Who's at Risk

All providers — violence is the #1 underreported EMS injury.

Common Injuries

Soft tissue injuries, bites, eye injuries, psychological trauma.

Key Stat: Studies suggest up to 90% of EMS violence incidents go unreported.
Prevention: Position yourself near exits. Trust your instincts. Report every incident — data drives policy change.

Other / Miscellaneous

Overview

Includes needle sticks, chemical exposures, repetitive strain from documentation and driving posture, and hearing loss from siren exposure.

Who's at Risk

Providers across all roles and experience levels.

Common Injuries

Bloodborne pathogen exposure, carpal tunnel, chronic hearing loss, skin conditions.

Key Stat: Hearing loss is one of the most overlooked long-term occupational hazards in EMS.
Prevention: Wear PPE consistently. Use ergonomic positioning while driving. Get regular hearing checks.

Shift Fatigue

12–24 hour shifts impair reaction time, judgment, and physical coordination — dramatically increasing injury risk toward the end of shift.

Unpredictable Lifts

Unlike warehouse workers, EMS providers can't control lift conditions. Every scene is different — floors, angles, patient size, and bystander help all vary.

Scene Hazards

Homes, roadsides, and public spaces present hazards that can't be engineered away. Awareness and preparation are the only defenses.

Chronic Stress Load

Psychological stress tightens muscles, impairs posture, and reduces body awareness — all of which contribute to physical injury over time.

Cervical Spine
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Cervical muscle strain from sudden patient movements
  • Cervical disc herniation from repetitive awkward positioning
  • Facet joint irritation
  • Tension headaches from sustained neck flexion during patient care
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers frequently work in cramped spaces — leaning over patients in back seats, kneeling in hallways, or looking down during prolonged airway management. Sustained neck flexion compresses cervical discs and strains posterior muscles. Heavy helmet and radio equipment add compressive load.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Pain or stiffness not resolving after 48–72 hours of rest
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness radiating into the arm or hand
  • Headaches originating at the base of the skull
  • Clicking or grinding with neck movement
These symptoms may indicate disc or nerve involvement. See a provider — do not push through.
Prevention Strategies
  • Keep your chin tucked — not jutted forward — during patient care
  • Reposition yourself rather than twisting your neck to see
  • Strengthen deep cervical flexors: chin tucks, neck isometrics
  • Take micro-breaks to roll and decompress the neck during long calls
  • Adjust stretcher height so you're not constantly looking down
Shoulders
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Rotator cuff strain or tear — supraspinatus most common
  • Shoulder impingement syndrome
  • AC joint sprain from falls or carrying heavy equipment
  • Bicep tendinopathy
  • SLAP tears from repetitive overhead or loading motions
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

Loading patients overhead into high ambulances, reaching across stretchers, carrying equipment bags, and CPR compressions all place repetitive high-load demand on the rotator cuff. The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body — and mobility comes at the cost of stability.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Pain when reaching overhead or behind your back
  • Weakness when lifting — unable to hold arm at shoulder height
  • Painful arc between 60–120 degrees of arm raise
  • Night pain that wakes you from sleep
Rotator cuff injuries worsen significantly if ignored. Early intervention prevents surgical outcomes.
Prevention Strategies
  • Strengthen rotator cuff: external rotation exercises with resistance bands
  • Never load a cot or equipment overhead solo — use proper technique or request help
  • Keep elbows close to the body during lifts
  • Stretch pecs and anterior shoulder to prevent forward shoulder posture
  • Avoid reaching across your body with a loaded arm
Lumbar Spine
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Lumbar disc herniation — L4-L5 and L5-S1 most common in EMS
  • Lumbar muscle and ligament sprain
  • Sacroiliac joint dysfunction
  • Lumbar facet syndrome
  • Degenerative disc disease accelerated by cumulative loading
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

The lumbar spine is the most injured region in EMS. Heavy, unpredictable lifts from flexed or rotated positions — especially with a loaded stretcher — create compressive and shear forces that exceed safe spinal load thresholds. Fatigue, poor lift mechanics, and time pressure multiply the risk.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Sharp pain during or after a lift that doesn't resolve within a shift
  • Pain radiating down one or both legs (sciatica)
  • Numbness or tingling in legs, feet, or groin
  • Inability to stand fully upright after bending
Loss of bladder or bowel control is a medical emergency — seek care immediately. Disc herniations and nerve compression require prompt evaluation.
Prevention Strategies
  • Hinge at the hips — not the waist — on every lift
  • Keep load close to your body at all times
  • Adjust stretcher height before lifting — never lift from ground level when avoidable
  • Strengthen the posterior chain: glutes, hamstrings, and erectors
  • Core stability: dead bugs, planks, bird dogs — not sit-ups
  • Use mechanical lift assist or additional crew for bariatric patients
Knees
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Patellar tendinopathy from repetitive kneeling on hard surfaces
  • Meniscus tears from twisting while supporting a loaded patient
  • MCL/LCL sprains from falls or lateral forces at uneven scenes
  • IT band syndrome from repetitive stair climbing
  • Prepatellar bursitis from prolonged kneeling
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers kneel on hard floors, concrete, and asphalt regularly during patient care. Stair climbing with loaded stair chairs places significant eccentric load on the patellar tendon and quadriceps. Pivoting while supporting a patient creates dangerous rotational forces on the knee joint.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Swelling around the kneecap or joint line
  • Locking or catching sensation in the knee
  • Inability to fully straighten or bend the knee
  • Pain descending stairs more than ascending
  • Instability or "giving way" feeling
Locking and instability suggest meniscal or ligament involvement — do not delay evaluation.
Prevention Strategies
  • Use a kneeling pad or kneel on one knee rather than both when working on the floor
  • Strengthen quads and glutes to protect the joint
  • Avoid twisting your knee while bearing weight
  • Communicate steps clearly with your partner during stair chair operations
  • Stretch hip flexors and calves — tightness above and below affects knee tracking
Wrists & Hands
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome from repetitive documentation and steering
  • De Quervain's tenosynovitis — thumb-side wrist pain from gripping
  • Scaphoid fracture from catching a fall with an outstretched hand
  • Trigger finger from repetitive gripping of equipment and bags
  • Hand lacerations and crush injuries at chaotic scenes
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

Sustained gripping of stretcher handles, stair chairs, and equipment bags — combined with hours of documentation on tablets or keyboards — creates repetitive strain on wrist tendons and the median nerve. Falls frequently result in wrist fractures as providers instinctively reach out to catch themselves.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Numbness or tingling in the thumb, index, or middle fingers — especially at night
  • Pain with wrist extension or gripping
  • Swelling or bruising after a fall — always rule out scaphoid fracture
  • Weakness in grip strength
Scaphoid fractures can appear normal on initial X-ray. Always follow up persistent wrist pain after a fall.
Prevention Strategies
  • Use ergonomic grips or gloves for equipment handling
  • Take wrist stretch breaks during documentation-heavy periods
  • Wrist flexor and extensor stretches — hold 30 seconds each direction
  • Strengthen grip and wrist extensors with resistance exercises
  • When falling, try to tuck and roll rather than reaching out
Ankles & Feet
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Lateral ankle sprain — most common EMS lower extremity injury
  • Plantar fasciitis from prolonged standing on hard surfaces
  • Achilles tendinopathy
  • Stress fractures from high-volume calls on hard surfaces
  • Metatarsalgia from poorly fitting boots
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers navigate uneven terrain, stairs, wet surfaces, and debris — often while carrying equipment or supporting patients. Long shifts on hard floors accelerate plantar fascia fatigue. Heavy, ill-fitting boots impair proprioception and ankle stability.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Ankle pain and swelling immediately after a misstep or fall
  • Heel pain worst with the first steps in the morning — plantar fasciitis hallmark
  • Achilles pain or tightness that worsens with activity
  • Localized bone pain that increases with activity
Ottawa Ankle Rules: if you can't bear weight or have bony point tenderness, get imaging.
Prevention Strategies
  • Wear properly fitted, supportive boots with ankle coverage
  • Replace boots every 12 months — outsole wear affects joint protection
  • Stretch calves and plantar fascia daily
  • Ankle stability exercises: single-leg balance, resistance band work
  • Use high-quality insoles if standing for prolonged periods
  • Slow down on approach to scenes — never rush across unknown terrain
