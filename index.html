Overexertion & Lifting

Overview

The leading cause of EMS injury. Repeated heavy lifts in awkward positions — stairwells, tight hallways, unstable surfaces — place extreme load on the spine and shoulders.

Who's at Risk

All providers, but especially those with 5+ years on the job due to cumulative wear.

Common Injuries

Lumbar disc herniation, muscle strains, rotator cuff tears.

Key Stat: A loaded stretcher can weigh 300–700+ lbs. EMS workers may perform 5–10 significant lifts per shift.
Prevention: Always adjust stretcher height before loading. Request lift assist when needed. Never rush a lift.

Slips, Trips & Falls

Overview

Scene environments are unpredictable — wet floors, uneven terrain, dark stairwells, ice, and debris all create fall hazards.

Who's at Risk

Providers responding at night or in adverse weather.

Common Injuries

Ankle sprains, knee ligament tears, wrist fractures, head injuries.

Key Stat: Falls account for a disproportionate share of lost work days compared to their overall frequency.
Prevention: Slow down on approach. Use a flashlight. Plant your feet before lifting. Wear slip-resistant footwear.

Vehicle Incidents

Overview

Both ambulance crashes and being struck while working near roadways. Intersection collisions are the #1 cause of EMS fatalities.

Who's at Risk

Drivers and providers working highway or MVC scenes.

Common Injuries

Whiplash, traumatic brain injury, fractures, fatalities.

Key Stat: EMS workers are 2.5× more likely to die in a vehicle incident than the average worker.
Prevention: Always wear your seatbelt in the ambulance — even in the back. At roadway scenes, position the unit as a barrier and wear high-visibility gear.

Struck by Object

Overview

Equipment falling from overhead storage, stretcher components releasing unexpectedly, or objects at chaotic scenes.

Who's at Risk

Providers working in tight quarters or with aging equipment.

Common Injuries

Hand and wrist fractures, eye injuries, lacerations, head trauma.

Key Stat: Ambulance interior equipment is responsible for a significant portion of struck-by incidents.
Prevention: Inspect all latches and locks at the start of every shift. Keep cabinets closed when the unit is in motion.

Assault & Violence

Overview

EMS providers are frequently exposed to patients or bystanders who are combative due to psychiatric crisis, intoxication, or conditions like hypoglycemia or hypoxia.

Who's at Risk

All providers — violence is the #1 underreported EMS injury.

Common Injuries

Soft tissue injuries, bites, eye injuries, psychological trauma.

Key Stat: Studies suggest up to 90% of EMS violence incidents go unreported.
Prevention: Position yourself near exits. Trust your instincts. Report every incident — data drives policy change.

Other / Miscellaneous

Overview

Includes needle sticks, chemical exposures, repetitive strain from documentation and driving posture, and hearing loss from siren exposure.

Who's at Risk

Providers across all roles and experience levels.

Common Injuries

Bloodborne pathogen exposure, carpal tunnel, chronic hearing loss, skin conditions.

Key Stat: Hearing loss is one of the most overlooked long-term occupational hazards in EMS.
Prevention: Wear PPE consistently. Use ergonomic positioning while driving. Get regular hearing checks.

Shift Fatigue

12–24 hour shifts impair reaction time, judgment, and physical coordination — dramatically increasing injury risk toward the end of shift.

Unpredictable Lifts

Unlike warehouse workers, EMS providers can't control lift conditions. Every scene is different — floors, angles, patient size, and bystander help all vary.

Scene Hazards

Homes, roadsides, and public spaces present hazards that can't be engineered away. Awareness and preparation are the only defenses.

Chronic Stress Load

Psychological stress tightens muscles, impairs posture, and reduces body awareness — all of which contribute to physical injury over time.

Cervical Spine
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Cervical muscle strain from sudden patient movements
  • Cervical disc herniation from repetitive awkward positioning
  • Facet joint irritation
  • Tension headaches from sustained neck flexion during patient care
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers frequently work in cramped spaces — leaning over patients in back seats, kneeling in hallways, or looking down during prolonged airway management. Sustained neck flexion compresses cervical discs and strains posterior muscles. Heavy helmet and radio equipment add compressive load.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Pain or stiffness not resolving after 48–72 hours of rest
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness radiating into the arm or hand
  • Headaches originating at the base of the skull
  • Clicking or grinding with neck movement
These symptoms may indicate disc or nerve involvement. See a provider — do not push through.
Prevention Strategies
  • Keep your chin tucked — not jutted forward — during patient care
  • Reposition yourself rather than twisting your neck to see
  • Strengthen deep cervical flexors: chin tucks, neck isometrics
  • Take micro-breaks to roll and decompress the neck during long calls
  • Adjust stretcher height so you're not constantly looking down
Shoulders
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Rotator cuff strain or tear — supraspinatus most common
  • Shoulder impingement syndrome
  • AC joint sprain from falls or carrying heavy equipment
  • Bicep tendinopathy
  • SLAP tears from repetitive overhead or loading motions
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

Loading patients overhead into high ambulances, reaching across stretchers, carrying equipment bags, and CPR compressions all place repetitive high-load demand on the rotator cuff. The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body — and mobility comes at the cost of stability.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Pain when reaching overhead or behind your back
  • Weakness when lifting — unable to hold arm at shoulder height
  • Painful arc between 60–120 degrees of arm raise
  • Night pain that wakes you from sleep
Rotator cuff injuries worsen significantly if ignored. Early intervention prevents surgical outcomes.
Prevention Strategies
  • Strengthen rotator cuff: external rotation exercises with resistance bands
  • Never load a cot or equipment overhead solo — use proper technique or request help
  • Keep elbows close to the body during lifts
  • Stretch pecs and anterior shoulder to prevent forward shoulder posture
  • Avoid reaching across your body with a loaded arm
Lumbar Spine
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Lumbar disc herniation — L4-L5 and L5-S1 most common in EMS
  • Lumbar muscle and ligament sprain
  • Sacroiliac joint dysfunction
  • Lumbar facet syndrome
  • Degenerative disc disease accelerated by cumulative loading
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

The lumbar spine is the most injured region in EMS. Heavy, unpredictable lifts from flexed or rotated positions — especially with a loaded stretcher — create compressive and shear forces that exceed safe spinal load thresholds. Fatigue, poor lift mechanics, and time pressure multiply the risk.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Sharp pain during or after a lift that doesn't resolve within a shift
  • Pain radiating down one or both legs (sciatica)
  • Numbness or tingling in legs, feet, or groin
  • Inability to stand fully upright after bending
Loss of bladder or bowel control is a medical emergency — seek care immediately. Disc herniations and nerve compression require prompt evaluation.
Prevention Strategies
  • Hinge at the hips — not the waist — on every lift
  • Keep load close to your body at all times
  • Adjust stretcher height before lifting — never lift from ground level when avoidable
  • Strengthen the posterior chain: glutes, hamstrings, and erectors
  • Core stability: dead bugs, planks, bird dogs — not sit-ups
  • Use mechanical lift assist or additional crew for bariatric patients
Knees
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Patellar tendinopathy from repetitive kneeling on hard surfaces
  • Meniscus tears from twisting while supporting a loaded patient
  • MCL/LCL sprains from falls or lateral forces at uneven scenes
  • IT band syndrome from repetitive stair climbing
  • Prepatellar bursitis from prolonged kneeling
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers kneel on hard floors, concrete, and asphalt regularly during patient care. Stair climbing with loaded stair chairs places significant eccentric load on the patellar tendon and quadriceps. Pivoting while supporting a patient creates dangerous rotational forces on the knee joint.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Swelling around the kneecap or joint line
  • Locking or catching sensation in the knee
  • Inability to fully straighten or bend the knee
  • Pain descending stairs more than ascending
  • Instability or "giving way" feeling
Locking and instability suggest meniscal or ligament involvement — do not delay evaluation.
Prevention Strategies
  • Use a kneeling pad or kneel on one knee rather than both when working on the floor
  • Strengthen quads and glutes to protect the joint
  • Avoid twisting your knee while bearing weight
  • Communicate steps clearly with your partner during stair chair operations
  • Stretch hip flexors and calves — tightness above and below affects knee tracking
Wrists & Hands
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome from repetitive documentation and steering
  • De Quervain's tenosynovitis — thumb-side wrist pain from gripping
  • Scaphoid fracture from catching a fall with an outstretched hand
  • Trigger finger from repetitive gripping of equipment and bags
  • Hand lacerations and crush injuries at chaotic scenes
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

Sustained gripping of stretcher handles, stair chairs, and equipment bags — combined with hours of documentation on tablets or keyboards — creates repetitive strain on wrist tendons and the median nerve. Falls frequently result in wrist fractures as providers instinctively reach out to catch themselves.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Numbness or tingling in the thumb, index, or middle fingers — especially at night
  • Pain with wrist extension or gripping
  • Swelling or bruising after a fall — always rule out scaphoid fracture
  • Weakness in grip strength
Scaphoid fractures can appear normal on initial X-ray. Always follow up persistent wrist pain after a fall.
Prevention Strategies
  • Use ergonomic grips or gloves for equipment handling
  • Take wrist stretch breaks during documentation-heavy periods
  • Wrist flexor and extensor stretches — hold 30 seconds each direction
  • Strengthen grip and wrist extensors with resistance exercises
  • When falling, try to tuck and roll rather than reaching out
Ankles & Feet
Common EMS Injuries & Prevention
Common Injuries
  • Lateral ankle sprain — most common EMS lower extremity injury
  • Plantar fasciitis from prolonged standing on hard surfaces
  • Achilles tendinopathy
  • Stress fractures from high-volume calls on hard surfaces
  • Metatarsalgia from poorly fitting boots
Why EMS Workers Are Vulnerable

EMS providers navigate uneven terrain, stairs, wet surfaces, and debris — often while carrying equipment or supporting patients. Long shifts on hard floors accelerate plantar fascia fatigue. Heavy, ill-fitting boots impair proprioception and ankle stability.

Warning Signs — When to Seek Care
  • Ankle pain and swelling immediately after a misstep or fall
  • Heel pain worst with the first steps in the morning — plantar fasciitis hallmark
  • Achilles pain or tightness that worsens with activity
  • Localized bone pain that increases with activity
Ottawa Ankle Rules: if you can't bear weight or have bony point tenderness, get imaging.
Prevention Strategies
  • Wear properly fitted, supportive boots with ankle coverage
  • Replace boots every 12 months — outsole wear affects joint protection
  • Stretch calves and plantar fascia daily
  • Ankle stability exercises: single-leg balance, resistance band work
  • Use high-quality insoles if standing for prolonged periods
  • Slow down on approach to scenes — never rush across unknown terrain
Section 03 — Technique & Ergonomics

Lifting & Patient Handling

Proper body mechanics save careers. Step through each technique and learn the exact sequence that protects your spine, shoulders, and knees on every call.

Step 1 of 5
Assess & Prepare

Before any lift begins, evaluate the scene. Know your patient's weight, the floor surface, and any obstacles. Confirm your team and assign roles out loud.

Verbally confirm patient weight with partner
Clear loose rugs, cords, and trip hazards from the path
Identify the destination and plan your route before lifting
If patient exceeds safe lift limit — call for more crew
Step 2 of 5
Stance & Position

Plant your feet shoulder-width apart, one foot slightly forward. Keep the load close to your center of mass. Never position yourself with feet together.

Feet shoulder-width apart, staggered front-to-back
Stretcher or patient as close to your body as possible
Toes pointed forward — no twisting at the hip
Both partners mirror each other's foot position
Step 3 of 5
Grip the Stretcher

Use a palm-up, full-hand power grip on the stretcher rail — not fingertip grip. Lock your wrists in a neutral position before initiating the lift.

Palm up, fingers and thumb fully wrapped
Wrists straight — no flexion or extension under load
Elbows close to the body, not flared outward
Confirm grip before calling the count
Step 4 of 5
Drive the Lift

On a synchronized count, extend the knees and hips together — do not lead with your back. The power comes from your legs. Back stays locked in neutral lordosis throughout.

Count out loud: "Ready — lift on three. One, two, three."
Push through your heels, not your toes
Back angle does not change — only legs extend
Do not jerk or bounce — smooth, controlled extension
Step 5 of 5
Move & Lower

Walk with short steps, keeping the load stable. To lower, reverse the process — bend knees, maintain back neutral, and communicate the lowering sequence with your partner.

Short steps — no shuffling or crossing feet
Communicate every direction change verbally
Lower with the same controlled count used to lift
Don't drop — controlled descent protects patient and crew

Step 1 of 5
Equipment Setup

Inspect the stair chair before use. Verify all locking mechanisms are secure, wheels locked, and safety belt is functional. Set it up adjacent to the patient.

Check frame integrity — no bent rails or loose bolts
Test wheel lock before patient contact
Position chair on the same level as the patient
Have safety belt accessible before transfer begins
Step 2 of 5
Patient Positioning

Transfer the patient to the chair using proper body mechanics. Secure the safety belt snugly across the lap. Confirm patient is centered and balanced before moving.

Never drag or slide patient — use a controlled transfer
Safety belt should be snug — not restrictive to breathing
Patient's arms remain in lap — no grabbing the frame
Confirm patient is comfortable before any movement
Step 3 of 5
Team Positions

Lead provider takes the top rear handles — facing down the stairs. Second provider grasps the front footrest — facing upstairs. Both providers maintain neutral spine before initiating descent.

Top provider: power grip on handles, back straight
Bottom provider: squat grip on footrest frame, never the footplate
Eye contact and verbal confirmation before first step
Third provider stands below to spot if available
Step 4 of 5
Controlled Descent

Move one step at a time on a verbal count. Keep the chair angled at approximately 45 degrees to the stairway. Never tilt beyond the point of control.

One step at a time — both feet planted before next step
Top provider calls each step: "Step — step — step"
Maintain consistent tilt — no sudden angle changes
If you lose control, hold in place and call for help
Step 5 of 5
Transfer & Recovery

At the bottom landing, return the chair to upright. Transfer patient to the stretcher using proper body mechanics. Assess patient and crew for any signs of strain or injury.

Stretch briefly if you felt any lower back strain
Document the move if it was unusually difficult
Return stair chair to apparatus — clean and re-inspect
Report any near-miss or equipment issues immediately

Step 1 of 5
Clear the Scene

Floor lifts demand maximum space. Move furniture, rugs, and obstacles to create a clear path of at least 3 feet on either side of the patient before bringing in equipment.

Move furniture — don't work around it
Remove loose rugs — major slip hazard during ground lifts
Create 3-foot clearance on each side of patient
Brief the patient on each step before contact
Step 2 of 5
Roll to Side

Log-roll the patient to their side before placing the scoop stretcher or backboard. Use a 3-person team when available. Maintain spinal alignment throughout the roll.

One provider controls the head and calls the count
Roll toward the dominant provider — synchronized
Do not allow the torso to twist independently of the hips
Hold the roll only as long as needed to place equipment
Step 3 of 5
Place & Stage Equipment

Slide the stretcher or scoop under the patient while held in the roll. Secure straps before returning the patient to supine. Position the stretcher for final loading direction.

Scoop stretcher preferred for ground-level patients
Secure at least 3 straps before full supine
Position stretcher head-end toward loading direction
Keep the stretcher on the floor — do not raise until outside
Step 4 of 5
Team Lift

All providers kneel on one knee adjacent to the patient. On a verbal count, lift simultaneously to knee height, then stand. Never lift with your back — drive through legs from the kneeling position.

Kneel — do not bend over the patient
Lift to knee height first, then stand in second movement
All providers must lift at the exact same time
Use 4 providers for patients over 200 lbs
Step 5 of 5
Height Adjustment

Once the patient is at waist height, activate the stretcher's height mechanism to full working height. Never carry a loaded stretcher at floor level through a scene.

Raise stretcher before moving through the scene
Lock height before movement — confirm lock audibly
Tilt patient head-up if tolerated — reduces venous pooling
Post-lift: assess both crew members for back discomfort

Step 1 of 5
Assign Roles

Before contact, the lead provider designates who controls the head/upper body and who controls the lower body. The head provider calls all counts and direction changes.

Designate the head provider — typically the heavier or taller of the two
Head provider is in command of all verbal cues
Lower provider never initiates movement independently
Confirm roles verbally before patient contact
Step 2 of 5
Choose Carry Method

Select the carry based on space constraints. Cradle carry for short distance/tight spaces. Extremity carry for alert, cooperative patients. Both require synchronized technique.

Cradle: one arm under knees, one under upper back — avoid neck
Extremity: head provider under axillae, lower provider holds knees
Never use carries for patients with suspected spinal injury
Keep carries under 20 feet — get equipment for longer distances
Step 3 of 5
Lift on Count

Both providers assume their grip positions simultaneously. On the head provider's count, both drive upward from the legs. Lift height is to waist — not shoulder level.

Grip firm before the count begins
Count: "Ready — lift on three. One, two, three."
Lift only to waist height — above waist is unnecessary strain
Both providers must reach full standing before moving
Step 4 of 5
Movement Mechanics

Walk in small, deliberate steps. The head provider faces the direction of travel and guides the lower provider verbally. Do not pivot or twist — change direction by stepping, not rotating the torso.

Head provider calls "left," "right," "step up" for every change
Lower provider mirrors pace — no independent acceleration
Short steps only — long strides destabilize the carry
Stop and reposition if either provider feels strain
Step 5 of 5
Controlled Lower

Lower simultaneously on a count. Bend knees, maintain back neutral, and lower the patient to the surface without releasing grip until the patient is fully settled.

Count down before lowering — never without warning
Both providers bend knees at the same rate
Do not release until patient is fully on the surface
Post-carry: check in with your partner — any pain or strain?

Step 1 of 5
Pre-Call Planning

When dispatch indicates a bariatric call, begin planning before arrival. Determine weight estimate, request additional resources, and identify specialized equipment needs.

Request mutual aid or additional crew during dispatch
Confirm bariatric stretcher is on the unit or request one
Know your agency's safe lift limit per provider (typically 50 lbs)
Plan for egress — narrow doorways may require additional equipment
Step 2 of 5
Equipment Confirmation

Before patient contact, confirm that all bariatric equipment is on scene and functional. Standard stretchers and carry devices are contraindicated above weight limits.

Bariatric stretcher rated to patient weight — confirm rating label
Bariatric stair chair if stairs are involved
Slider boards and friction-reducing sheets for repositioning
Do not improvise with standard equipment — risk to patient and crew
Step 3 of 5
Team Assembly

Minimum 4 providers for any bariatric lift. Assign positions and roles before contact. Designate a single lift commander who calls all counts. Assign a dedicated patient communicator.

4 providers minimum — head, torso, hips, lower extremities
One commander calls all lifts — no one moves independently
Assign one person to talk to and monitor the patient throughout
Briefing before contact: every provider knows their role
Step 4 of 5
Modified Lift Mechanics

Bariatric lifts require modified technique due to uneven weight distribution. Focus on sharing the load equally. Use mechanical assists where available — do not rely on brute strength.

Use friction-reducing devices before any repositioning
Lift in stages — floor to knee, knee to stretcher height
Mechanical lift devices (Hoyer, EMS Rabbit) preferred if available
If the lift feels unsafe — stop, reassess, and request more resources
Step 5 of 5
Recovery Protocol

Post-bariatric call, all providers should assess themselves for strain. Document the lift in your report. Bariatric calls have the highest provider injury rate — recovery and reporting are non-optional.

All crew members do a physical self-check after the call
Any pain or discomfort — report to supervisor immediately
Document patient weight, crew size, and equipment used in the PCR
Stretching immediately post-call reduces delayed-onset strain
