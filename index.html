Overexertion & Lifting

Overview

The leading cause of EMS injury. Repeated heavy lifts in awkward positions — stairwells, tight hallways, unstable surfaces — place extreme load on the spine and shoulders.

Who's at Risk

All providers, but especially those with 5+ years on the job due to cumulative wear.

Common Injuries

Lumbar disc herniation, muscle strains, rotator cuff tears.

Key Stat: A loaded stretcher can weigh 300–700+ lbs. EMS workers may perform 5–10 significant lifts per shift.
Prevention: Always adjust stretcher height before loading. Request lift assist when needed. Never rush a lift.

Slips, Trips & Falls

Overview

Scene environments are unpredictable — wet floors, uneven terrain, dark stairwells, ice, and debris all create fall hazards.

Who's at Risk

Providers responding at night or in adverse weather.

Common Injuries

Ankle sprains, knee ligament tears, wrist fractures, head injuries.

Key Stat: Falls account for a disproportionate share of lost work days compared to their overall frequency.
Prevention: Slow down on approach. Use a flashlight. Plant your feet before lifting. Wear slip-resistant footwear.

Vehicle Incidents

Overview

Both ambulance crashes and being struck while working near roadways. Intersection collisions are the #1 cause of EMS fatalities.

Who's at Risk

Drivers and providers working highway or MVC scenes.

Common Injuries

Whiplash, traumatic brain injury, fractures, fatalities.

Key Stat: EMS workers are 2.5× more likely to die in a vehicle incident than the average worker.
Prevention: Always wear your seatbelt in the ambulance — even in the back. At roadway scenes, position the unit as a barrier and wear high-visibility gear.

Struck by Object

Overview

Equipment falling from overhead storage, stretcher components releasing unexpectedly, or objects at chaotic scenes.

Who's at Risk

Providers working in tight quarters or with aging equipment.

Common Injuries

Hand and wrist fractures, eye injuries, lacerations, head trauma.

Key Stat: Ambulance interior equipment is responsible for a significant portion of struck-by incidents.
Prevention: Inspect all latches and locks at the start of every shift. Keep cabinets closed when the unit is in motion.

Assault & Violence

Overview

EMS providers are frequently exposed to patients or bystanders who are combative due to psychiatric crisis, intoxication, or conditions like hypoglycemia or hypoxia.

Who's at Risk

All providers — violence is the #1 underreported EMS injury.

Common Injuries

Soft tissue injuries, bites, eye injuries, psychological trauma.

Key Stat: Studies suggest up to 90% of EMS violence incidents go unreported.
Prevention: Position yourself near exits. Trust your instincts. Report every incident — data drives policy change.

Other / Miscellaneous

Overview

Includes needle sticks, chemical exposures, repetitive strain from documentation and driving posture, and hearing loss from siren exposure.

Who's at Risk

Providers across all roles and experience levels.

Common Injuries

Bloodborne pathogen exposure, carpal tunnel, chronic hearing loss, skin conditions.

Key Stat: Hearing loss is one of the most overlooked long-term occupational hazards in EMS.
Prevention: Wear PPE consistently. Use ergonomic positioning while driving. Get regular hearing checks.

Shift Fatigue

12–24 hour shifts impair reaction time, judgment, and physical coordination — dramatically increasing injury risk toward the end of shift.

Unpredictable Lifts

Unlike warehouse workers, EMS providers can't control lift conditions. Every scene is different — floors, angles, patient size, and bystander help all vary.

Scene Hazards

Homes, roadsides, and public spaces present hazards that can't be engineered away. Awareness and preparation are the only defenses.

Chronic Stress Load

Psychological stress tightens muscles, impairs posture, and reduces body awareness — all of which contribute to physical injury over time.
